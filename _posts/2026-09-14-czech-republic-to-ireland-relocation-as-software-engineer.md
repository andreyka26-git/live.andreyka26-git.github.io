## **Introduction**

I worked for Microsoft for 4 years and have already written [an article about why I decided to leave](https://live.andreyka26.com/i-left-microsoft-after-4-years), as well as [an article about my Google and Snowflake offers](https://live.andreyka26.com/i-got-an-offer-at-google-and-snowflake-in-2025).

This article is about the relocation process from Prague (Czech Republic) to Dublin (Ireland) as a Software Engineer hired by Snowflake. Besides that, I will cover the details and pros/cons of living in both of these countries, which might help you choose one.




<br>

## **Relocation Process**

One important point: since russia invaded Ukraine, I didn't finish my work permit properly for the Czech Republic and had to switch to a Special Temporary Protection visa for Ukrainians. 
The problem with it is that your time on it does not count towards a residence permit / citizenship - therefore I didn't really see a stable future in the country. There is no way to switch to a work permit inside the Czech Republic.

In 2024 I called the Irish and Dutch embassies in the Czech Republic to ask whether it is even possible to get a work permit in case I find an employer. I remember the Netherlands said yes - just find an employer, and Ireland said: "no, for Ukrainians we do not issue any work permits, there is not even a button in the system for that, you just come with your passport". (`Spoiler - you actually CAN do that, I'm stating that as an IRP and Critical Skills visa holder`).

When I got an offer from Snowflake - due to the above information, I wanted to make sure that I would get a proper visa at that time, before doing anything else.

How it works in Ireland:



* you apply for a **Critical Skills Employment Permit** (**CSEP**, stamp 1 visa). This gives you the right to work for a specific employer for 2 years (generally)
* **Irish Residence Permit (IRP)** - this is generally issued for one year, and it gives you the right to stay in the country.

It sounds a bit confusing to me, but yes, these are 2 different documents and you obtain them separately as well. To get an IRP you need to be in Ireland and have the Critical Skills permit + a bunch of other documents.




<br>

### **Critical Skills Visa Application**

Snowflake, like most big tech companies, hires third-party partners that handle visa applications for employees. In autumn 2025 they asked for a bunch of documents, and I uploaded everything that was required from my side under their supervision.

In 1-2 months (end of autumn) I got the Critical Skills visa, so I could start the relocation.




<br>

### **Ireland - here we go**


[![alt_text](/assets/2026-09-14-czech-republic-to-ireland-relocation-as-software-engineer/image3.png "image_tooltip")](/assets/2026-09-14-czech-republic-to-ireland-relocation-as-software-engineer/image3.png "image_tooltip"){:target="_blank"}


Snowflake provided initial money for a hotel / temporary accommodation and flight tickets. I booked the hotel closest to the office, and in January I was in Dublin. 
 
**Critical Skills** != **Residence permit** - so how can you enter the country then? At the airport they put a stamp in your passport saying that you have 3 months of stay in Ireland to get your IRP. To be precise, you just have to book an appointment within 90 days. The appointment itself can be later. Note, though, that appointment registration (online) is possible only from within Ireland.

I felt a bit of pressure, and my first goal was to get the IRP. But it requires other documents, including address confirmation (lease agreement with the landlord / bills with your name). So I scheduled an IRP appointment within the first week, because the queue can be very long, delaying it by 2-3 weeks.




<br>

### **Home finding**

You've probably heard that the housing situation in Dublin is crazy. For me it took 2-3 weeks to find an apartment, given that I was single, had no pets, no kids, no need for parking, and could live in a very small studio without much comfort. 
 
I had probably 5-7 viewings, and at every single one I saw 10-30 people going for the same apartment. Potential tenants were sending documents right away, asking to be picked.

(this is my neighbour in the photo)

Maybe I was lucky, but I got a very small studio (12-14 m^2) with shared washing machines for 1600 eur monthly. The apartment is of decent quality and within 20 minutes' walking distance of work, which was amazing for me. One downside - the shared washing machines are paid, so every cycle costs me something like 5-6 eur, and sometimes the meters are full, so you literally cannot dry or wash until the landlord empties them.




<br>

### **PPSN (Personal Public Service Number)**

This is sort of a tax number or social security number. Without it being reported to your employer, your salary is taxed at 52% (emergency tax). So you want to get it as soon as possible.

For a PPSN you need:



* Passport
* Address confirmation (lease agreement / bills with your name)
* Employment contract

I have heard rumors that it is possible to apply with a temporary address confirmation document, for example a letter on hotel letterhead. But I was not able to check that because my PPSN appointment was rescheduled and the hotel confirmation had already expired by that time. So I used my long-term lease.

Don't forget to report your PPSN to your employer's payroll and to Revenue myAccount so that you are not emergency taxed.

I got my PPSN in January, and it was my first very pleasant surprise about Ireland. As a Slavic person who lived in Ukraine and the Czech Republic, I usually expect a very bad attitude and a bad mood on the day of going to any government institution. Usually people there are pretty rude, unhappy, and sometimes just hate you. But Ireland is a completely different case. I was happy returning from the place where I got my PPSN; people were very kind, smiling, explaining extra stuff, asking if I needed a translation, etc. This is a completely different experience, and I'm very grateful for that.




<br>

### **IRP (Irish Residence Permit)**

I was lucky again because I got my appointment in February. Nothing special - I just prepared all the documents (lease agreement, employment confirmation, passport, insurance) and went to the appointment. I remember I didn't actually need all the documents I brought; it was just me panicking, as I had only 3 months for that.

Now I could exhale: I had a place to live and documents to be / work here legally. It felt nice at that point.

The next step for most engineers here is to live like that for 21 months and apply for a stamp 4 visa, which is sort of "permanent residence" that makes you independent of your employer, as for now I can only work for Snowflake and no one else.

Then in 3 more years (5 in total) - apply for citizenship. However, right now there are moves to make it 8 years, which is okay; for me it does not change much - I love the country and don't plan to move out anyway.

## **Healthcare**

Maybe some of you remember my experience with a failed appendicitis surgery in Prague back then. Since then I have been very picky, careful, and anxious about healthcare.

As I understand it, Ireland has a hybrid approach: private healthcare / insurance, and a public one.




<br>

### **Public healthcare**

Under a certain net income you are eligible for a Medical Card, which makes pretty much everything healthcare-related free. It is not a static threshold - it depends on your expenses as well - but roughly it means 2k eur NET a month.

With that card you have free GP visits, tests, and specialist visits, along with emergencies.




<br>

### **Private healthcare**

For income above 2k eur net a month, you pay for most of the stuff (except emergency).

There are a bunch of private hospitals with specialists, as well as clinics with GPs. How it usually works: you have insurance that pays part of the expenses for you. However, for specialist appointments you generally need to go to a GP first. 
I guess this is because Europe in general has huge problems with healthcare budgeting.

Therefore, queues to specialists and labs might be shorter for you compared to public ones.

Note, though, that with private insurance I **was almost never** fully reimbursed: sometimes it is 50% coverage, sometimes it's 80%, very rarely it is 100%. Some things are not covered at all, e.g. vaccination renewals, some blood tests, etc., even though they were referred by a GP.




<br>

### **Emergency**

I am so glad I didn't have to check, but my understanding is that the emergency room and service is a flat rate (100 eur) for everything you might need to save your life (or to get an appendectomy xD).




<br>

### **My experience with healthcare**

The first thing is how GPs work here. I was very confused, as some people said that there **IS** an assigned GP and I need to find one and get assigned. Then a bunch of clinics and GPs told me that they don't have the concept of an "assigned" one.

My understanding for now is that if you have private insurance and you don't have an assigned one, you go to whatever clinic and GP you want and pay for that. Then, in case any specialist asks you, you can refer to the GP you visited last.

 
Snowflake provides private health and dental insurance (VHI) for employees. However, it usually does not cover everything, as I mentioned. GP visits are covered at something like 60-70%, physiotherapist appointments are covered at 50%, whereas dental (hygiene) was fully covered and I didn't even need to fill in a claim.

The problem is that a lot of the time you need to submit a claim manually, check whether it is approved, and if not, ask the medical institution for more documents. One time I didn't get my ultrasound covered; another time, at the same hospital, it was covered fully. My vaccination renewal was not covered at all (e.g. tetanus every 10-15 years). However, it was ALL referred by a GP.

 
Worth mentioning one more time - I'm so pleasantly surprised by how kind and nice people are here, making sure you understood everything, not angry at you whatsoever.

For now I have an amazing GP, very intelligent and knowledgeable (which is apparently rare nowadays xD), and a bunch of private and public clinics to go to if something happens. So I'm pretty happy with the situation.

## **Taxes**

Painful topic. For all big tech enjoyers - congrats, your stocks are taxed on vesting (52%) + taxed on capital gains (33%). If you invest in an ETF, you have "unrealised gains tax" that taxes you every 8 years even if you don't sell.

As for salary taxation - it is progressive.




<br>

## **Properties**

In my opinion it is much better than in continental Europe in case you want to stay here. Why so? Yes, rent prices are extremely high in Ireland, BUT if you decide to buy an apartment, most of the time your mortgage is CHEAPER than renting (excluding the down payment).

So in case you want to stay here for more than a few years, it makes sense to just go for a mortgage and save a lot on rent. Worth noting: apartment prices in Dublin and Prague are very close to each other; however, the salaries are not.




<br>

## **Czech Republic vs Ireland**

Given that I spent four years in Prague and have already spent almost one year in Dublin, I have some things to compare. Disclaimer: it is SOLELY my opinion.

Please don't perceive it as hate or anything like that. I am deeply thankful to all European and non-European countries that introduced temporary protection and let me and other Ukrainians work and stay there. From my side, I always tried to be a good resident, be grateful, never committed crimes, always paid taxes, etc.

This information is only for people who are considering relocating to the Czech Republic vs Ireland and want to see some opinions.




<br>

### **Language**

In the Czech Republic you will need Czech for a comfortable life. Working for Microsoft does not require Czech, and most of my colleagues didn't know and didn't learn Czech. However, any time you need to go to a Czech POINT, post office, healthcare institution, or government institution - believe me, you will struggle a lot if you don't speak any Slavic language. Besides that, your mental state will degrade a lot, because you usually get hated much more for not speaking Czech.

In Ireland, English is spoken everywhere.

Personally, I didn't learn Czech, as I didn't know whether I wanted to stay long term or not. Since I already had a B2 English level, Ireland was an amazing choice from that standpoint.




<br>

### **People**

This is my observation, but I believe that Slavic people are a bit more rude (including me and Ukrainian people), a bit more nationalistic, and in terms of migration policy, more right-wing. I have seen some evidence of it throughout my life.

Ireland is much more chill; I felt a huge difference, and in general people are more talkative, nice, and kind. I love that, and I want to be such a person as well (working on changing my "Slavic face" expression to something that has at least some signs of a smile).




<br>

### **Healthcare**

In general, EU healthcare is very similar. In CZ they have more centralized semi-public, semi-private insurance called VZP, and generally speaking you don't have to deal with claims; it is mostly paid automatically, and if it is not - it is just not - you are going to pay in cash.

I don't think there is a huge quality difference. There are always bad doctors and good doctors, and you try to find the good ones. There is no real punishment for mistakes (negligence, just not following protocols, etc.) made by doctors (I have heard a lot of stories). Unfortunately, this is an underbudgeting issue that I hope humanity will take into account.

It shouldn't be the case that I care about a feature rollout more than doctors care about doing a procedure or prescribing medications, but it is, unfortunately.




<br>

### **Taxes**

The Czech Republic is the clear winner here. First of all, it simply has lower taxes for both salaries AND stocks. Apart from that, CZ has a rule that if you hold vested stock for more than 3 years, you can sell without paying capital gains tax. However, the amount of stock you will get in CZ compared to Ireland is dramatically different - go and check levels.fyi for MS, for example.




<br>

### **Software Engineering Market**

Ireland is the clear winner. There are so many more big companies - almost the whole FAANG abbreviation - and tier 2 companies, whereas big tech representation in Prague is only Microsoft (I mean big dev centers, not just sales offices for 50 people). There are still a bunch of tier 2 companies, like JetBrains, Pure Storage, etc., but Ireland still has so much more.

I would say that, as a software engineer, it is pretty much the same. I didn't feel a difference in how much I save (except stocks, which is better in Ireland), nor a difference in quality of life.

### **Naturalization**

Prague requires 10 years + the Czech language + historical/cultural questions. Ireland (as of now) requires 5 years, with no tests for now except a "Good character" verification (convictions incl. abroad, driving offences, court cases, cautions).

However, an [amendment has been proposed](https://www.gov.ie/en/department-of-justice-home-affairs-and-migration/press-releases/government-approval-secured-for-the-priority-drafting-of-the-irish-nationality-and-citizenship-amendment-bill-2026/) that will add language exams at least (potentially the Irish language as well) and extend the required residence time from 5 to 8 years. Which is still better than in the Czech Republic.
